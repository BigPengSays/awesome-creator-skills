## Standard Prompt Compiler

Compile only requirements that become pixels. Answer these fields in order.

### 1. Abstraction Blueprint

Treat the subject illustration as priority 1. When literal likeness conflicts with a strong authored cartoon, keep only the identity anchors and choose the stronger cartoon. Record internally:

First run `Subject Recognition and Anchoring`. Identify all visually prominent or interacting people, creatures, or objects that plausibly form the intended subject. Lock a roster as `S1`, `S2`, and so on, plus left/right/front/back order, relative scale, pose/action relationship, contact and occlusion, and held or shared objects. Treat distant incidental bystanders as background, but when a prominent or interacting figure is ambiguous, preserve it rather than silently deleting it. Unless the user explicitly selects one figure, the roster count is immutable: never merge two figures into one hybrid or borrow one figure's face, hair, clothing, limbs, or accessories for another.

When `style_preset` is active, bind the matching fixed style block after roster locking and before the numbered blueprint fields. Translate its roles onto the actual subject—human, group, creature, object, or mixed roster—using only observed identity anchors. The preset controls how the subject is redesigned, never which subject content exists. When no preset is active, derive all blueprint choices normally.

1. `identity anchors`: keep only 3-5 high-information traits for each rostered subject and bind every anchor to its subject ID. Prefer one dominant silhouette or hair-mass trait, one facial-spacing or head-shape trait, one pose/action relation, and at most one signature accessory or object. An anchor is a semantic cue, not permission to reproduce its exact contour or decoration. Do not inventory clothing panels, prints, embroidery, jewelry, folds, wrinkles, skin texture, lighting, or anatomical detail.
2. `source severance`: convert the anchors into a short text-only character brief, then rebuild from that brief as if the photograph were unavailable. Deliberately abandon the original cutout contour, crop logic, facial map, body proportions, modeled volume, shadow pattern, garment construction, decorative placement, and photographic edge continuity. Reusing the source silhouette and merely simplifying its pixels fails; the new subject must be impossible to obtain by masking, tracing, posterizing, or mosaicking the source.
3. `caricature mutation`: select one identity anchor as the dominant visual hook and exaggerate 2-3 traits around it. Make at least one major proportion mutation and one major silhouette mutation, plus a third change among facial spacing, limb thickness, pose angle, object scale, negative-space rhythm, or accessory scale. Push these changes far enough to feel authored, asymmetrical, awkward, blunt, strange, or charmingly ugly; loose recognition comes only from the anchor set, not from preserving the source anatomy.
4. `mass plan`: redraw each subject from 5-9 flat interlocking masses, preferably 5-7, with one unmistakable dominant mass, one counter-mass, and a few supporting blocks. Reduce the head and face to a coarse mask of a few stepped clusters; reduce hair to one or two masses, each garment to one to three broad planes, and limbs to blunt structural shapes. Replace volume primarily with contrasting flat colors, cutouts, negative space, straight-edged facets, and hard silhouette breaks. Reserve checkerboard for an indispensable non-skin curved surface or an ambiguous contact between planes. Delete every non-anchor print, ornament, seam, fold, strand, pore, wrinkle, highlight, fingernail, subtle expression, and decorative micro-feature instead of translating it into pixels.
5. `authored style lock`: render the subject as an adult 1980s bitmap editorial caricature or underground terminal illustration—angular, industrial, graphic, slightly grotesque, and visibly designed from scratch. Favor uneven visual weight, purposeful asymmetry, compressed anatomy, abrupt stepped contours, mask-like facial construction, expressive negative space, low facet counts, straight runs, chamfers, wedges, rectangles, and blunt polygons. Treat curves as high-risk detail channels: default to no curves; when the subject becomes unreadable without one, allow only one or two large stepped arcs built from a few straight segments. Forbid flowing contours, repeated rounded features, realistic pixel portraiture, polished game-sprite anatomy, anime/chibi proportions, kawaii mascot language, round friendly children's-book shapes, decorative fashion illustration, clean vector-cartoon outlines, and generic schoolbook or clip-art character design.
6. `hand topology`: for every visible hand, bind left/right ownership, wrist attachment, palm orientation, contact or occlusion, and any clearly visible finger count before simplification. Use a readable blocky palm with anatomically plausible finger grouping. If the grid cannot support separate fingers, use an intentional mitten or grouped silhouette with no false finger tips; never create extra or missing fingers, fused hands, detached palms, inverted joints, impossible wrist connections, or hand-object penetration.
7. `pixel topology`: choose one global square base cell `p`, approximately `short canvas edge / 384`, for the entire interface. Use an approximately `30 x 30` planning grid only as the subject's contour/feature complexity budget, then build every subject shape as complete integer-aligned runs of the same `p x p` cells used by UI and text. Construct with horizontal/vertical runs, deliberate stair diagonals, chamfers, wedges, and low-sided polygons. Use a curve-free geometry recipe whenever possible; any indispensable curve becomes one coarse stepped arc assembled from 3-6 straight segments, never a smooth or continuously varying contour. Permit no half cells, stretched or rotated cells, sub-cell fragments, shifted rows, broken checker patterns, accidental cracks, isolated debris, mixed pixel scales, or smooth transition pixels. Do not render or resize an intermediate image.

Before prompt compilation, run eight rejection tests once. `Roster integrity`: every anchored subject remains distinct, correctly ordered, and recognizable through its own anchors. `Hand integrity`: every visible arm reaches the correct wrist and hand, and every depicted finger grouping is plausible. `Silhouette divergence`: the rebuilt subject must not align closely with the photograph's continuous outer contour, proportions, and pose. `Detail amputation`: no source garment construction, pattern, ornament, fold, jewelry inventory, facial modeling, or local contour survives unless it is the single declared signature anchor. `Filter removal`: after mentally removing UI and CRT texture, the subject must still read as a separately authored flat caricature, never a photograph underneath pixelation. `Style independence`: the subject must read as an angular adult bitmap editorial illustration, not realistic pixel portraiture, a polished game sprite, anime/chibi art, cute mascot art, children's-book illustration, or generic clip art. `Skin cleanliness`: every face and exposed-skin region is built only from solid palette planes with no checkerboard, stipple, hatch, or spotted texture. `Curve austerity`: geometry is predominantly straight, faceted, and low-sided; no recipe contains flowing contours or a chain of small variable arcs, and any indispensable stepped arc is large, sparse, and structurally necessary. If a test fails, correct the blueprint once by deleting fidelity, checker texture, or curves and increasing flat geometric redesign instead of adding texture, detail, or another generation loop.

Assign broad tonal planes with bold solid palette-color collisions. Add a regular 50% darkest/lightest checkerboard only to the smallest necessary non-skin region: a curved object needing one optical middle tone or touching planes whose depth relation is otherwise unreadable. Zero checkerboard is valid. Never place it on a face or exposed skin, and never use it as all-over texture. Never rely on realistic rendering, cutout, pixel filter, auto-pixelate, blur, antialiasing, gradient transitions, smooth curves, or photographic edge continuity.

### 2. Attention and Interface Geometry

- Place the single wallpaper composition—one figure or one anchored group—off-center using the recipe; its final silhouette occupies 60%-80% when possible and never below 50%, including after window occlusion.
- Give windows obvious large/medium/small tiers; the dominant is about 1.8x a medium and the smallest no more than half a medium. Spread centers across at least two quadrants, preferably three, and never stack three similar windows on one side.
- Overlap selected windows by 5%-20%, cross the subject boundary, and keep overall visual mass balanced. Never frame the primary subject or place it in a portrait/bitmap-viewer window.
- Use square borders, title bars, close boxes, scroll tracks, primitive tables/charts, short French bitmap labels, the top menu/drop-down, and one cursor.
- Include 1-3 magnifier-like feature-extraction windows. Isolate recognizable local elements and show each as a tight crop. Magnify by allocating more shared-grid cells to the crop, never by enlarging its cell beyond `p`. With multiple extractions, vary both outer dimensions and aspect ratios; no two share the same size or proportions.

### 3. Color and Pixel Construction

- Apply the resolved palette only. Use its lightest and darkest colors for field/ink contrast and checkerboard endpoints; use remaining colors as intentional flat planes, title bars, panels, icons, or signal accents. The selected polarity recipe determines whether the dominant field is light, dark, or locally split; no polarity is the universal default.
- Permit no color outside the palette, alpha, intermediate computed gray, gradient, translucent glow, or antialiasing. Prefer separate solid palette planes; when a true optical half-tone is structurally necessary, use one compact regular darkest/lightest checkerboard region. Keep every face and exposed-skin region solid and checker-free. Do not scatter checks as noise, use them decoratively, or cover the figure with them.
- Build subject edges, UI geometry, icons, and 5x7, 6x8, or 7x9 glyphs from the same global square cells and coarse straight runs, stair diagonals, chamfers, wedges, and low-sided polygons. Simulate bloom only as palette-bound hard-cell expansion; do not use checker fringe on skin. Curves are disabled by default and limited to one or two indispensable coarse stepped arcs in any recipe that permits them.

### 4. CRT Signal Surface

Cover the frame with dense palette-bound scanlines, sparse noise, one-cell misregistration, short vertical persistence, and one restrained sync disturbance. Force radial barrel distortion inside the outer 10% on every side with nearest-cell displacement: corners compress more than edge centers, the top menu baseline and side rails visibly bow, and no finished frame remains rectangular or flat. Keep the inner 80% stable and show no physical monitor, bezel, or room.
